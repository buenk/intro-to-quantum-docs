**Logical gates** model elementary computational steps in digital electronic circuits. For example:

| $x$ | NOT$(x)$ |
|:---:|:---:|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 |

| $xy$ | AND$(x,y)$ | OR$(x,y)$ | XOR$(x,y)$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 00 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 01 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| 10 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| 11 | 1 | 1 | 0 |

Algebraically (modulo 2): $\mathrm{NOT}(x) = x+1$, $\mathrm{AND}(x,y) = x \cdot y$, $\mathrm{XOR}(x,y) = x+y$.

Any **Boolean function** $f\colon \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$ can be expressed using just AND, OR, NOT. For example, equality:

| $xy$ | EQ$(x,y)$ |
|:---:|:---:|
| 00 | 1 |
| 01 | 0 |
| 10 | 0 |
| 11 | 1 |

$$
\mathrm{EQ}(x,y) = 1 \iff x = y \iff (x=0 \text{ and } y=0) \text{ or } (x=1 \text{ and } y=1)
$$

This gives the formula:

$$
\mathrm{EQ}(x,y) = \mathrm{OR}\big(\mathrm{AND}(\mathrm{NOT}(x), \mathrm{NOT}(y)),\ \mathrm{AND}(x,y)\big)
$$

On the **Boolean circuit**:

![Boolean circuit for EQ(x,y): x and y each feed a NOT gate, the two NOT outputs feed an AND gate, x and y also feed a second AND gate, and both AND outputs feed an OR gate whose output is EQ(x,y)](figures/L04-p01-1.png)

Since this construction works for any Boolean function, $\{\mathrm{AND},\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{NOT}\}$ is a **universal set of logical gates**. In fact, just $\{\mathrm{AND},\mathrm{NOT}\}$ or even just $\{\mathrm{NAND}\}$ would also do the job.

## Single-qubit gates

We saw in Lecture 1 that a general one-qubit operation is of the form $U = \begin{pmatrix} a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix}$ where $a,b,c,d \in \C$ are such that $\abs{a}^2 + \abs{b}^2 = 1$, $\abs{c}^2 + \abs{d}^2 = 1$, $a\bar{c} + b\bar{d} = 0$. This is equivalent to $U^\dagger U = I$, meaning $U$ is unitary.

Thus, a **one-qubit gate** is any $2\times 2$ unitary.

Expanded in the computational basis:

$$
U = \sum_{i,j=0}^{1} U_{i,j} \ket{i}\!\bra{j}
$$

$$
\ket{\psi_\text{out}} = VU\ket{\psi_\text{in}}
$$

*(← time)*

Graphical notation:

![Circuit: a single gate U with input wire labelled j and output wire labelled i, and a circuit with input state psi_in, gate U, then gate V, giving psi_out, with a time arrow pointing right](figures/L04-p02-1.png)

:::example
The **Hadamard gate** $H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ changes basis between the computational basis $\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}$ and the Hadamard basis $\{\ket{+},\ket{-}\}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H\ket{0} &= \ket{+} & H\ket{+} &= \ket{0} \\
H\ket{1} &= \ket{-} & H\ket{-} &= \ket{1}
\end{aligned}
$$

![Circuit symbol: a box labelled H on a wire](figures/L04-p02-2.png)
:::

:::example
The **Pauli gates**:

![Circuit symbols: boxes labelled I, X, Y, Z on wires](figures/L04-p02-3.png)

$$
I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\qquad
X = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{NOT operation}}
\qquad
Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\qquad
Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

You can check that $X^2 = Y^2 = Z^2 = I$ and $XY = -YX = iZ$.
:::

Note: They are closely related to quaternions.

## Multi-qubit quantum gates

A **two-qubit gate** is a $4\times 4$ unitary matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= \sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{1} U_{ij,kl} \overbrace{\ket{i,j}}^{\text{output}} \underbrace{\bra{k,l}}_{\text{input}} \\
&= \sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{1} U_{ij,kl} \ket{i}\!\bra{k} \otimes \ket{j}\!\bra{l}
\end{aligned}
$$

*(← time)*

![Circuit: a two-qubit gate U with input wires k and l on the left and output wires i and j on the right; time arrow to the right, space (registers) arrow pointing down](figures/L04-p03-1.png)

:::example
$$
\ket{00}\!\bra{00} + \ket{01}\!\bra{01} + \ket{10}\!\bra{10} + \ket{11}\!\bra{11} =
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & & & \\ & 1 & & \\ & & 1 & \\ & & & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{matrix} \ket{00} \\ \ket{01} \\ \ket{10} \\ \ket{11} \end{matrix}
$$

is the 2-qubit identity operation.
:::

Similarly, an $n$-qubit gate is a $2^n \times 2^n$ unitary. It has $n$ input and $n$ output qubits.

## Local operations

If a 1-qubit gate is applied **locally** on one of two qubits, we can write it as a 2-qubit unitary:

$$
\underbrace{\overset{1}{U} \otimes \overset{2}{I}}_{\text{1st qubit}}
\qquad\qquad
\overset{1}{I} \otimes \underbrace{\overset{2}{U}}_{\text{2nd qubit}}
$$

![Circuit: gate U on wire 2 of two wires; space (registers) arrow pointing down](figures/L04-p03-3.png)

## Single- and multi-qubit measurements

The **standard basis measurement** of a qubit is denoted by

$$
\ket{\psi} = \alpha_0\ket{0} + \alpha_1\ket{1}
\qquad
\xrightarrow{\ \abs{\alpha_0}^2\ }\ 0
\qquad
\xrightarrow{\ \abs{\alpha_1}^2\ }\ 1
$$

![Circuit: input state psi enters a measurement box, whose double-wire output is 0 or 1; annotations: input state, double wire denotes the classical output bit](figures/L04-p04-1.png)

We can describe such measurement using the operators (now vectors) $\{\bra{0},\bra{1}\}$. Indeed, the outcome $i\in\{0,1\}$ occurs with probability $p_i := \abs{\braket{i|\psi}}^2$. If you want to also keep the post-measurement state, you can instead use the operators ($2\times 2$ matrices) $\{\ket{0}\!\bra{0}, \ket{1}\!\bra{1}\}$. Let $\Pi_i = \ket{i}\!\bra{i}$. Then

$$
p_i := \norm{\Pi_i\ket{\psi}}^2 = \norm{\ket{i}\braket{i|\psi}}^2 = \underbrace{\norm{\ket{i}}^2}_{1} \cdot \abs{\braket{i|\psi}}^2
$$

and the state after the measurement is

$$
\ket{\psi_i} = \frac{\Pi_i\ket{\psi}}{\sqrt{p_i}} = \frac{\ket{i}\!\braket{i|\psi}}{\abs{\braket{i|\psi}}} \underset{\text{equal up to a global phase}}{\sim} \ket{i}.
$$

This is called **projective measurement** since $\Pi_i$ are projectors. The same formulas work for any orthonormal basis.

If you measure only one out of two qubits, you can tensor the measurement operators with $I$:

$$
\{\bra{i}\otimes I\}
$$

![Circuit: state psi on two wires, the first wire measured, the second wire left in state psi_i](figures/L04-p04-2.png)

$$
p_i := \norm{(\bra{i}\otimes I)\ket{\psi}}^2
$$

$$
\underbrace{\ket{\psi_i}}_{\text{remaining state on the 2nd qubit}} = \frac{(\bra{i}\otimes I)\ket{\psi}}{\sqrt{p_i}}
$$

$$
\{I\otimes\bra{i}\}
$$

![Circuit: state psi on two wires, the second wire measured, the first wire left in state psi_i](figures/L04-p04-3.png)

$$
p_i := \norm{(I\otimes\bra{i})\ket{\psi}}^2
$$

$$
\ket{\psi_i} = \frac{(I\otimes\bra{i})\ket{\psi}}{\sqrt{p_i}}
$$

## Quantum circuits

An $n$-qubit **quantum circuit** is a sequence of $n$-qubit unitary operations, followed by the measurement in the standard basis:

![Quantum circuit: n qubits each starting in 0, passing through unitaries U_1, U_2, ..., U_m, then each measured to give classical outputs i_1, i_2, ..., i_n](figures/L04-p05-1.png)

The output is an $n$-bit string $i_1 i_2 \ldots i_n \in \{0,1\}^n$, with probability

$$
\left|\underbrace{\bra{i_1 i_2 \ldots i_n}}_{\text{output}}\ \underbrace{U_m \cdots U_2 U_1}_{\text{computation}}\ \underbrace{\overbrace{\ket{00\ldots 0}}^{\ket{0}^{\otimes n}}}_{\text{input}}\right|^2 .
$$

:::example
SWAP$\ket{i}\otimes\ket{j} = \ket{j}\otimes\ket{i}$

![Circuit: two qubits starting in 0; an X gate on the first wire, then a SWAP gate, then a Hadamard gate on the first wire, then both wires measured; dotted lines mark the states |0>|0>, |1>|0>, |0>|1>, |+>|1>](figures/L04-p05-2.png)

$01$ w.p. $\frac12$

$11$ w.p. $\frac12$

This circuit is equivalent to

![Circuit: X on the first wire and H on the second wire, then SWAP, then both measured](figures/L04-p05-3.png)

and

![Circuit: SWAP, then H and measurement on the first wire, X and measurement on the second wire](figures/L04-p05-4.png)
:::

:::theorem[Fact]
Any intermediate measurement can be deferred to the end. Measurements in any other orthonormal basis can always be converted to the standard basis measurement.
:::

## Locality and uniformity

Computation is **local** if it consists of elementary operations that act only on a few qubits at a time.

:::example[for bits]
Any Boolean function $f\colon\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ can be computed using logical gates (e.g., AND and NOT) with at most two input bits.
:::

We would like quantum circuits to be local as well because:

- it is hard to make more than two quantum systems to interact simultaneously,
- the hardware might have only a finite number of different types of interactions available (in particular, they act only on some finite number of qubits),
- we cannot afford to store $2^n\times 2^n$ matrices to describe the algorithm (e.g., when $n=50$, there are not enough atoms in the universe to write down each matrix entry, even if we use one atom per entry).

To solve a computational problem, we need not just a single circuit, but a family of circuits - one circuit for every input size of the problem (e.g., we cannot simply use the circuit that adds 3-bit numbers to add 5-bit numbers). But where do we get these circuits in the first place? They are generated by some other (classical) algorithm.

A **uniform** family of circuits is one that can be produced by a deterministic Turing machine in polynomial time.

This excludes the possibility of generating an (exponential size) look-up table that stores all possible answers.

## Quantum algorithm

A quantum algorithm is an infinite family $C_1, C_2, \ldots$ of quantum circuits, where $C_n$ acts on $n$ qubits and consists of a finite sequence of 1-qubit and 2-qubit gates: $C_n = (U_1, U_2, \ldots, U_{L(n)})$ where $L(n)$ denotes the number of gates in the circuit. The map $n\mapsto C_n$ must be efficiently computable (in polynomial time on a deterministic Turing machine). In particular, $L(n)$ must be polynomial in $n$.

![Quantum circuit on five qubits starting in 0, built from 2x2 gates (small boxes) and 4x4 gates (tall boxes) acting on neighbouring wires, followed by a measurement on every wire; annotations: 4x4 matrix, 2x2 matrix](figures/L04-p07-1.png)

This is how a typical quantum algorithm might look like. All gates act on at most two (neighbouring) qubits and are easy to specify since they involve at most $4\times 4 = 16$ complex numbers. To fully specify the algorithm, one must provide at most $16\cdot L(n)$ complex numbers, which is only polynomial in $n$, plus at most two $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$-bit numbers per gate to specify on which qubits it acts on. The total amount of information is still polynomial in $n$ and hence in principle manageable.

In contrast, the overall unitary transformation implemented by the algorithm is described by a $2^n\times 2^n$ matrix, which is too big to even write down. This also implies that not all unitary $n$-qubit transformations can be implemented by a $\mathrm{poly}(n)$-size quantum circuit.
