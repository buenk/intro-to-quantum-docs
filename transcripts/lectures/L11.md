To understand the order finding problem, we need to recap some basic number theory -- modular arithmetic.

## Extended Euclidean algorithm

It can be used to find the greatest common divisor and the modular multiplicative inverse.

Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ be integers (one of them not 0). The **greatest common divisor** of $a$ and $b$, $\gcd(a,b)$, is the largest positive integer that divides both $a$ and $b$.

Assume $a \geq b \geq 0$. Note that $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(a-b, b)$. In fact, $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(a - mb, b)$ for any $m \in \mathbb{Z}$. Let's take $m$ as large as possible such that $a - mb \geq 0$, i.e. $m = \lfloor a/b \rfloor$ (or `a//b` in Python notation). Then $a - mb$ is the reminder of $a$ modulo $b$, i.e., $a \bmod b$ (or `a % b` in Python).

<!-- page 2 -->

The Euclidean algorithm consists in repeatedly applying the substitution

$$
(a, b) := (b, \underbrace{a \% b}_{\text{mod}})
$$

until the smallest integer becomes 0, which happens very fast. At this point $a$ is equal to gcd of the original two numbers.

:::example
($a = 66$, $b = 15$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
66 \bmod 15 &= 6 \\
15 \bmod 6 &= 3 \\
6 \bmod \underline{3} &= 0 \qquad \gcd(66,15) = 3
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{(a_k, b_k) := (b_{k-1}, a_{k-1} \bmod b_{k-1})}
$$
:::

The **extended** version of Euclidean algorithm finds a solution to Bézout's identity by also keeping track of the quotients. Namely, it finds integers $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that

$$
x \cdot a + y \cdot b = d
$$

where $d = \gcd(a,b)$. Once we find the gcd, we have an equation $a_k - m_k \cdot b_k = d$, which expresses $d$ as an integer linear combination of $a_k$ and $b_k$. We can substitute $b_k$ from the previous equation $a_{k-1} - m_{k-1} \cdot b_{k-1} = b_k$ to express $d$ as an integer linear combination of $a_{k-1}$ and $b_{k-1} = a_k$. We repeat until we get back to $a_1$ and $b_1$.

:::example
($a = 66$, $b = 15$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
66 - 4 \cdot 15 &= 6 \\
15 - 2 \cdot 6 &= 3 = \gcd(a,b)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
15 - 2 \cdot (66 - 4 \cdot 15) = \underbrace{-2 \cdot 66}_{132}\, + \underbrace{9 \cdot 15}_{135} = 3
$$

$x = -2$, $y = 9$
:::

## Multiplicative inverse

If $\gcd(a,b) = 1$, the integers $a$ and $b$ have no non-trivial common divisor and are called **relatively prime**. In such case Bézout's identity reads

$$
x \cdot a + y \cdot b = 1.
$$

If we take both sides modulo $a$, we get

$$
y \cdot b \equiv 1 \pmod a,
$$

meaning $y$ is the **modular multiplicative inverse** of $b$ modulo $a$. We can write this as $y = b^{-1}$.

:::example
($a = 66/3 = 22$, $b = 15/3 = 5$): Dividing the previous Bézout identity $-2 \cdot 66 + 9 \cdot 15 = 3$ by $3$ we get $-2 \cdot 22 + 9 \cdot 5 = 1$. Modulo $22$ gives $9 \cdot 5 \equiv 1 \pmod{22}$, so $5^{-1} \equiv 9 \pmod{22}$. Indeed, $5 \cdot 9 = 45 \equiv 1 \pmod{22}$.
:::

## Multiplicative order

Hence, if $\gcd(a,b) = 1$, we can both multiply and divide by $b$ modulo $a$ (or the other way round). What numbers do we get in this way? I.e., how does the set $\{b^k \bmod a : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ look like?

:::example
($a = 22$, $b = 5$):

$$
\begin{array}{ccccccccc}
b^0 & b^1 & b^2 & b^3 & b^4 & b^5 & b^6 & \dots \\
1 & 5 & 3 & 15 & 9 & 1 & 5 & \dots
\end{array}
$$

Hence, $b^k = b^{k+5}$ for any $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
:::

![Circle diagram: the values 1, 5, 3, 15, 9 (b^0, b^1, b^2, b^3, b^4 = b^{-1}) placed in order around a closed loop](figures/L11-p03-1.png)

Let $a, N \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $N \geq 1$ and $\gcd(a,N) = 1$. The **multiplicative order** of $a$ modulo $N$ is the smallest positive integer $r$ such that $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$.

<!-- page 4 -->

The reason such $r$ always exists when $\gcd(a,N) = 1$ is because there are at most $N$ possible values that $a^k$ can take modulo $N$. Hence, the sequence $a^0, a^1, a^2, \dots$ has to repeat at some point, i.e.,

$$
a^k \equiv a^{k+r} \pmod N,
$$

for some $r \geq 1$ and all sufficiently long $k$. But since $a$ is invertible, we can multiply both sides by $(a^{-1})^k = a^{-k}$ and get $1 \equiv a^r \pmod N$.

This also follows from a more general result known as **Euler's Theorem**: if $a$ and $N$ are relatively prime then

$$
a^{\varphi(N)} \equiv 1 \pmod N,
$$

where $\varphi(N)$ is the number of integers $c \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ such that $\gcd(a,c) = 1$. The security of the RSA cryptosystem is based on the assumption that it is hard to compute $\varphi(N)$ without knowing the prime factorization of $N$.

## Factoring and order finding problems

**Factoring**: Assume $N = pq$ where $p$ and $q$ are odd primes (the general case can be handled with a little more effort).

- Input: $N$
- Output: $p$ and $q$

**Order finding**: Assume $N \geq 2$ is an integer and $a \in \{2, \dots, N-1\}$ is such that $\gcd(N,a) = 1$.

- Input: $a$ and $N$
- Output: the smallest integer $r \geq 1$ such that $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$, i.e., the order of $a$ modulo $N$.

<!-- page 5 -->

## How to factor using order finding

**Factor**$(N)$:

```
a ∈_R {2, ..., N-1}
g = gcd(N, a)
if g ≠ 1
    return (g, N/g)
else
    r = Order(a, N)
    if r is even, let x = a^{r/2} else restart
    if x + 1 ≢ 0 (mod N)
        return (gcd(N, x+1), gcd(N, x-1))
    else restart
```

*(Annotations: at "return (g, N/g)": "We were extremely lucky and guessed a factor". At "r = Order(a, N)": "Call the quantum subroutine for order finding".)*

Analysis of the algorithm:

:::theorem[Fact]
If $N = pq$ and $a \in_R \{2, \dots, N-1\}$ is such that $\gcd(N,a) = 1$ then with probability $> 1/2$

- $\text{Order}(a,N)$ is even AND
- $a^{r/2} + 1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod N$
:::

In other words, each run fails and has to restart with probability $< 1/2$. If we try $k$ times, all $k$ attempts fail with probability $< 2^{-k}$ which is extremely small.

If we made it till the very end, why are

$$
\gcd(N, x+1) \quad \text{and} \quad \gcd(N, x-1)
$$

the factors of $N$?

<!-- page 6 -->

Let $N = pq$, where $p$ and $q$ are odd primes, and assume we guessed an $a \in \{2, \dots, N-1\}$ such that

- $r = \text{Order}(a, N)$ is even AND
- $a^{r/2} + 1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod N$.

Let $x = a^{r/2}$. Since $r$ is the order of $a$,

$$
a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N
$$

which is the same as $x^2 - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod N$. We can factor this polynomial as

$$
(x-1)(x+1) \equiv 0 \pmod N. \tag{*}
$$

Note that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x - 1 &\not\equiv 0 \pmod N && \text{by minimality of } r, \\
x + 1 &\not\equiv 0 \pmod N && \text{by the 2nd assumption.}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the condition $(*)$ is equivalent to

$$
(x-1)(x+1) = kpq,
$$

for some integer $k$. Since neither $x-1$ or $x+1$ is a multiple of $N$, and $N = pq$, computing

$$
\gcd(N, x-1) \quad \text{and} \quad \gcd(N, x+1)
$$

will find $p$ and $q$. Either $p \mid (x-1)$ and $q \mid (x+1)$ or the other way round.

## Order finding = Period finding

An alternative way to look at the problem of finding the order of $a$ modulo $N$, i.e., computing $\text{Order}(a,N)$ is to find the **period** of the sequence

$$
a^0, a^1, a^2, a^3, \dots \pmod N
$$

Fourier transform and phase estimation are the right tools for this.

<!-- page 7 -->

Let us now connect the period / order finding problem to what we discussed in the previous lesson.

Consider the function $f_a : \mathbb{N} \to \{0, \dots, N-1\}$ given by

$$
f_a(x) = a^x \bmod N.
$$

Note that $f_a$ is periodic, with period at most $N$. Also, note that

$$
f_a(0) = f_a(k)
$$

is equivalent to

$$
a^k \equiv 1 \pmod N,
$$

so **the period of $f_a$ is equal to the order of $a$**.

## Period finding

Let us consider the more general problem of finding the period of some arbitrary function $f : \mathbb{N} \to \{0, \dots, N-1\}$ which is promised to be periodic, i.e., there is some $r \geq 1$ such that

$$
f(x + r) = f(x) \qquad \forall x.
$$

We have the ability to evaluate this function in superposition on any input.

In the case of $f_a$, we can use a reversible classical circuit for implementing $a^x$ (this can be done using repeated squaring and integer multiplication based on the binary digits of $x$). More generally, we can imagine having an oracle $U_f$ for computing $f$ and work from there.

<!-- page 8 -->

## Algorithm

Let $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^n$ (truncate $\mathbb{N}$ to $n$ bits; $N = 2^n$) and $U_f$ be its bit oracle

![Circuit: box $U_f$ with inputs $\ket{x}$, $\ket{y}$ (each an n-qubit register) and outputs $\ket{x}$, $\ket{y \oplus x}$](figures/L11-p08-0.png)

The quantum circuit is just like for Simon's algorithm, but with the second Hadamard transform $H^{\otimes n}$ replaced by $\text{QFT}^\dagger$. It is also identical to the phase estimation circuit.

![Circuit: first register $\ket{0^n}$ goes through $H^{\otimes n}$, then $U_f$, then $\text{QFT}^\dagger$, then a measurement giving $z \in \{0,1\}^n$; second register $\ket{0^n}$ goes into $U_f$ and the output is measured and discarded. Points along the circuit are labelled 1 to 6.](figures/L11-p08-1.png)

1. $\ket{0^n}\ket{0^n}$
2. $\ket{+}^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}$
3. $\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{2^n-1} \ket{x}\ket{f(x)}}_{\text{the uniform superposition over all } x \text{ and the corresponding } f(x)}$
4. Let $y_0 \in \{0,1\}^n$ be the measurement outcome when measuring the 2nd register. The post-measurement state is

$$
\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \ket{x_0 + kr} \right) \ket{y_0}
$$

![Plot of f(x) against x: a sawtooth function with period r; the points x_0, x_0+r, x_0+2r, ... where f(x) = y_0 are marked on the x-axis up to 2^n - 1](figures/L11-p08-2.png)

where

- $x_0 \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}$ is the least value s.t. $f(x_0) = y_0$,
- $r \in \{1, \dots, N-1\}$ is the period of $f$,
- $m = \lfloor 2^n / r \rfloor$ is the number of $x$ s.t. $f(x) = y_0$.

The state in the first register has periodic structure while $y_0$ itself is not useful for determining $r$.

<!-- page 9 -->

4. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \ket{x_0 + kr}$
5. Recall that, for any $x \in \{0, \dots, 2^n-1\}$,

$$
\text{QFT}^\dagger \ket{x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{z=0}^{2^n-1} \omega^{-xz} \ket{z}
$$

where $\omega = \exp(2\pi i / 2^n)$. Applying this on the state from the previous step gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \text{QFT}^\dagger \cdot \ket{x_0 + kr}
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{z=0}^{2^n-1} \omega^{-(x_0 + kr)z} \ket{z} \\
&= \sum_{z=0}^{2^n-1} \omega^{-x_0 z} \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \left( \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \omega^{-krz} \right) \ket{z}
\end{aligned}
$$

6. The probability of observing outcome $z \in \{0,1\}^n$ in the final measurement is just the $|\cdot|^2$ of the amplitude in front of $\ket{z}$:

$$
p(z) = \frac{1}{m 2^n} \left| \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \omega^{-krz} \right|^2
$$

The plot of $p(z)$, for $z \in \{0, \dots, 2^n-1\}$, looks something like this. It has $r$ peaks, each of height roughly $1/r$, located at integer multiples of $2^n/r$. Indeed, note that $\omega^{-krz} = \exp(-2\pi i\, krz / 2^n)$, so if $z = \ell \cdot 2^n/r$, for some $\ell \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $2\pi i\, krz/2^n = 2\pi i\, k\ell$ and $\omega^{-krz} = 1$ and $p(z) = \frac{1}{m 2^n} \cdot m^2 = m/2^n \simeq 1/r$ since $m = \lfloor 2^n/r \rfloor$. How do we recover $r$ from the measurement outcome $z$?

![Plot of p(z) for z from 0 to 2^n: r sharp peaks of height 1/r at z = 0, 2^n/r, 2·2^n/r, ..., (r-1)·2^n/r](figures/L11-p09-1.png)

<!-- page 10 -->

The general procedure involves finding a continued fraction expansion for $z/2^n$. Just to get the main idea, let us consider the very special case when the period $r$ itself is a power of 2: $r = 2^u$, for some $u \in \{0, \dots, n\}$. Then

$$
m = \lfloor 2^n / r \rfloor = \lfloor 2^{n-u} \rfloor = 2^{n-u} = 2^n / r \tag{*}
$$

and the probability to get measurement outcome $z = \ell \cdot 2^n/r$ is exactly $1/r$ for all $\ell \in \{0, \dots, r-1\}$ since

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(z) &= \frac{1}{m 2^n} \left| \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \omega^{-krz} \right|^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{m 2^n} \left| \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \exp\left( -\frac{2\pi i}{2^n} \cdot kr \cdot \ell \cdot \frac{2^n}{r} \right) \right|^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{m 2^n} \left| \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \underbrace{\exp(-2\pi i \cdot k \cdot \ell)}_{1} \right|^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{m 2^n} \cdot m^2 = \frac{m}{2^n} = 1/r \qquad \text{by } (*).
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence we do not get any other outcomes than $z = \ell \cdot 2^n/r$ for some $\ell \in \{0, \dots, r-1\}$. To estimate $r$ from such $z$, note that $z/2^n \in [0,1)$ is given by

$$
z/2^n = \ell/r = \ell/2^u
$$

since we assumed that $r = 2^u$. Since $\ell \in \{0, \dots, r-1\}$ is uniformly random, with probability $1/2$ it is odd so the binary expansion of $z/2^n = \ell/2^u$ looks as follows:

$$
\ell/2^u = 0.\overbrace{\underbrace{1011}_{u \text{ bits}}00\dots0}^{n \text{ bits}}
$$

where the last non-zero bit is at position $u$. From this $u$ we can infer $r$ since $r = 2^u$. The case of a general $r$ is more complicated and requires a continued fraction expansion for $z/2^n$.

<!-- page 11 -->

Another way to think about it, which does not use period finding but directly connects to phase estimation:

$$
U\ket{x} = \ket{ax \bmod N}
$$

is a permutation matrix, so it is diagonal in the Fourier basis and its eigenvalues are of the form $\exp(2\pi i\, k/\ell)$ where $\ell$ is the length of some cycle. In particular, $U\ket{1} = \ket{a}$, $U\ket{a} = \ket{a^2}$, etc. is the cycle that goes through all powers of $a$, so the corresponding eigenvalue is $e^{2\pi i / r}$ where $r$ is the order of $a$ modulo $N$.

We can get $r$ by running phase estimation on $\ket{1}$ (which is not an eigenvector itself, but has overlap $1/r$ with all eigenvectors, such as the one that is a uniform superposition of all powers of $a$).

Note that $\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{2^n-1} \ketbra{i}{i} \otimes U^i$ is the bit oracle for $f_a(x) = a^x \pmod N$, so that's why the algorithm is the way it is and we are literally just using phase estimation.
