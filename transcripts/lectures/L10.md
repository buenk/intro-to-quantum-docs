This and the following lecture are dedicated to deriving Shor's algorithm for factoring integers. In this lecture we will cover quantum phase estimation (QPE) and the quantum Fourier transform (QFT). The algorithm for order finding and factoring will be covered in the next lecture.

![Diagram: Breaking RSA, from Factoring, from Order finding, from Phase estimation, from QFT (each arrow pointing up)](figures/L10-p01-1.png)

Let's recall Deutsch's algorithm and the phase kick-back trick for converting bit oracles to phase oracles.

Let $f : \{0,1\} \to \{0,1\}$ and $U_f \ket{x,y} = \ket{x, y \oplus f(x)}$, for all $x, y \in \{0,1\}$, be the bit oracle for $f$. Deutsch's algorithm determines $f(0) \oplus f(1)$ or whether $f$ is constant or balanced. For concreteness, let's take $f(x) = x$, so $f(0) \oplus f(1) = 0 \oplus 1 = 1$, i.e., $f$ is balanced. For this choice of $f$, $U_f \ket{x,y} = \ket{x, y \oplus x}$, i.e.,

![Circuit: U_f maps |x>,|y> to |x>,|y xor x>, so U_f equals CNOT](figures/L10-p01-2.png)

Since $f(0) \oplus f(1) = 1$, Deutsch's algorithm outputs 1:

![Circuit: Hadamard on the first qubit (|0>), CNOT controlled by it targeting the second qubit (|->), Hadamard, measurement giving 1; steps 1 to 4 marked](figures/L10-p01-3.png)

1. $\ket{0}\ket{-}$
2. $\ket{+}\ket{-} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{0}\ket{-} + \ket{1}\ket{-})$
3. $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{0}\ket{-} - \ket{1}\ket{-}) = \ket{-}\ket{-}$
4. $\ket{1}\ket{-}$ (because $X\ket{-} = -\ket{-}$)

What if the second qubit is $\ket{+}$ instead of $\ket{-}$?

![Circuit: same as before but with the second qubit in state |+>, first qubit measured giving 0; steps 1 to 4 marked](figures/L10-p01-4.png)

1. $\ket{0}\ket{+}$
2. $\ket{+}\ket{+}$
3. $\ket{+}\ket{+}$
4. $\ket{0}\ket{+}$

<!-- page 2 -->

Here is how we can think about this. Note that

![Circuit equality: CNOT equals a controlled-X](figures/L10-p02-1.png)

$$
\begin{cases} X\ket{+} = \ket{+} \\ X\ket{-} = -\ket{-} \end{cases}
$$

where $\ket{+}$ and $\ket{-}$ are the eigenvectors of $X$. Given an eigenvector of $X$ as a quantum state, Deutsch's algorithm determines which eigenvector it is. Equivalently, it determines the corresponding eigenvalue --- if the measurement outcome is $b_1 \in \{0,1\}$ then the eigenvalue is $(-1)^{b_1}$ and the eigenstate was $\ket{(-1)^{b_1}}$. Note that this procedure preserves the eigenstate --- indeed the final state is $\ket{b_1}\ket{(-1)^{b_1}}$. This is the same as measuring an observable on one of its eigenvectors!

This idea can be generalized to an arbitrary basis. Let $V \in U(2)$ be an arbitrary unitary basis change, and let $U = V X V^\dagger$ be $X$ in a different basis. Since $X = \ketbra{+}{+} - \ketbra{-}{-}$, $U = \ketbra{\psi_0}{\psi_0} - \ketbra{\psi_1}{\psi_1}$ where $\ket{\psi_0} = V\ket{+}$ and $\ket{\psi_1} = V\ket{-}$ are the eigenvectors of $U$. We can generalize the algorithm as follows: for any $b_1 \in \{0,1\}$

![Circuit: Hadamard on the first qubit (|0>), controlled-U targeting the second qubit (|psi_{b_1}>), Hadamard, measurement giving b_1; steps 1 to 4 marked](figures/L10-p02-2.png)

$$
U\ket{\psi_{b_1}} = (-1)^{b_1}\ket{\psi_{b_1}}
$$

1. $\ket{0}\ket{\psi_{b_1}}$
2. $\ket{+}\ket{\psi_{b_1}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0}\ket{\psi_{b_1}} + \ket{1}\ket{\psi_{b_1}}\right)$
3. $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0}\ket{\psi_{b_1}} + \ket{1}(-1)^{b_1}\ket{\psi_{b_1}}\right)$

   $= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + (-1)^{b_1}\ket{1}\right)\ket{\psi_{b_1}} = \ket{(-1)^{b_1}}\ket{\psi_{b_1}}$
4. $\ket{b_1}\ket{\psi_{b_1}}$

Again, we determine the eigenvalue of $\ket{\psi_{b_1}}$ without destroying the state itself (it belongs to the basis in which we measure it).

This idea can be generalized beyond $\pm 1$ eigenvalues to an arbitrary eigenvalue $e^{i\varphi}$, $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$.

## Quantum phase estimation

Let $U \in U(d)$ and $\ket{\psi} \in \C^d$ be an eigenvector of $U$:

$$
U\ket{\psi} = e^{2\pi i \cdot b}\ket{\psi}, \quad \text{for some } b \in [0,1).
$$

**Problem:** Given access to a subroutine for controlled-$U$ and one copy of $\ket{\psi}$, determine $b$ (i.e., the phase / the eigenvalue of $\ket{\psi}$).

We already solved the case when $e^{2\pi i b} \in \{1, -1\}$, so $b \in \{0, 1/2\}$, or in binary $b \in \{0.0, 0.1\}$. In other words, $b = 0.b_1$ and we determined $b_1 \in \{0,1\}$. How about 2-bit accuracy? If $b = 0.b_1 b_2$, how do we find $b_1, b_2 \in \{0,1\}$?

Note that $U^2\ket{\psi} = e^{2\pi i \cdot 2b}\ket{\psi} = e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.b_2}$ since $2b = 10_2 \cdot 0.b_1 b_2 = b_1.b_2$ and $e^{2\pi i} = 1$. Hence, by using $U^2$ instead of $U$, we can determine $b_2$. But how about $b_1$?

Let's make the following general observation: if $b = 0.b_1 b_2 \ldots b_n$ then

$$
U^{2^k}\ket{\psi} = e^{2\pi i \cdot 2^k \cdot b}\ket{\psi}
$$

where

$$
2^k \cdot b = \underbrace{10\ldots 0_2}_{k} \cdot 0.b_1 b_2 \ldots b_n = \underbrace{b_1 b_2 \ldots b_k}_{\text{integer part}} . \underbrace{b_{k+1} b_{k+2} \ldots b_n}_{\text{fractional part with } n-k \text{ digits}}
$$

so $e^{2\pi i \cdot 2^k \cdot b} = e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.b_{k+1} \ldots b_n}$.

![Circuit: Hadamard on the first qubit (|0>), then controlled-U^{2^k} on the second qubit (|psi>); the first qubit ends in |phi_{n-k}>; steps 1 to 3 marked](figures/L10-p03-1.png)

1. $\ket{0}\ket{\psi}$
2. $\ket{+}\ket{\psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0}\ket{\psi} + \ket{1}\ket{\psi}\right)$
3. $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0}\ket{\psi} + \ket{1} e^{2\pi i \cdot 2^k \cdot b}\ket{\psi}\right)$

   $= \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.b_{k+1} \ldots b_n}\ket{1}\right)}_{\ket{\varphi_{n-k}}} \ket{\psi}$

![Bloch sphere with axes x, y, z: the state (1/sqrt2)(1, e^{i phi}) lies on the equator at angle phi from the x axis, with the equator circle highlighted](figures/L10-p03-2.png)

<!-- page 4 -->

Let's see what happens if we produce both the state with $k=0$ and $k=1$ when $n=2$, and see how we can recover $b_1$ from this.

![Circuit: two qubits in |0> each with a Hadamard; the first controls U^2 and the second controls U on |psi>; outputs labelled |phi_1> = and |phi_2> =](figures/L10-p04-1.png)

$$
\ket{\varphi_1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.b_2}\ket{1}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + (-1)^{b_2}\ket{1}\right)
$$

$$
\ket{\varphi_2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.b_1 b_2}\ket{1}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + (-1)^{b_1 . b_2}\ket{1}\right)
$$

We can first recover $b_2$ the same way as before --- by applying $H$ on the first qubit. Once we have $b_2$, we can remove it from the second state as follows: let $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}$ and note that

$$
S^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{so} \quad \begin{aligned} (S^\dagger)^{b_2}\ket{0} &= \ket{0} \\ (S^\dagger)^{b_2}\ket{1} &= (i^{-b_2})\ket{1} \end{aligned}
$$

Hence,

![Circuit: the two-qubit phase estimation circuit followed by a highlighted dashed box QFT^dagger (H on the first qubit giving b_2, controlled-S^dagger, H on the second qubit giving b_1, then a swap); an arrow from the annotation (1/sqrt2)(|0> + (-1)^{b_1}|1>) points to the second wire; outputs |b_1>, |b_2> reordered in the right order, and |psi>](figures/L10-p04-2.png)

$\ket{b_1}, \ket{b_2}$ reordered in the right order

The **inverse** of the highlighted circuit is called the **quantum Fourier transform** (QFT). So the 2-qubit QFT is

![Circuit: 2-qubit QFT in a dashed box labelled QFT; inputs |b_1>, |b_2> are swapped, then H on the new second wire, controlled-S, and H on the first wire; outputs |phi_1> = 0.b_2 and |phi_2> = 0.b_1 b_2](figures/L10-p04-3.png)

where $\ket{\varphi_k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i \cdot \overbrace{2^{n-k} \cdot b}^{0.b_{n-k+1} \ldots b_n}}\ket{1}\right)$. More generally,

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{b_1 b_2 \ldots b_n} = \ket{\varphi_1} \otimes \ket{\varphi_2} \otimes \ldots \otimes \ket{\varphi_n}.
$$

and the circuit can be obtained recursively.

The general quantum phase estimation circuit for estimating the phase of an eigenvalue of $U$ up to $n$ binary digits of precision looks as follows:

![Circuit: n control qubits in |0> each with a Hadamard; a dashed box of controlled powers U^{2^{n-1}}, ..., U^{2^1}, U^{2^0} acting on |psi>; control wires labelled |phi_1>, ..., |phi_{n-1}>, |phi_n> enter QFT^dagger; outputs measured giving b_1, ..., b_{n-1}, b_n; |psi> returned](figures/L10-p05-1.png)

Here is how you can think of the highlighted part. If the first $n$ qubits are in the computational basis state $\ket{a_1 a_2 \ldots a_n}$, for some $a_i \in \{0,1\}$, the total power of $U$ is

$$
a_1 2^{n-1} + a_2 2^{n-2} + \ldots + a_{n-1} 2^1 + a_n 2^0 = a
$$

where $a \in \{0, \ldots, 2^n - 1\}$ is the integer whose binary representation is $a_1 a_2 \ldots a_n$. Hence this part implements the following operation:

$$
\sum_{a=0}^{2^n - 1} \ketbra{a}{a} \otimes U^a
$$

We can abbreviate the quantum phase estimation circuit as follows:

![Circuit: |0^n> through H^{tensor n}, then a-controlled-U^a on |psi>, then QFT^dagger (the wire labelled n qubits), then measurement giving b_1 ... b_n; |psi> returned](figures/L10-p05-2.png)

This is somewhat reminiscent of Simon's algorithm, except for $\mathrm{QFT}^\dagger$ instead $H^{\otimes n}$ and $a$-controlled-$U$ instead of the bit oracle $U_f$ for some $f$.

## Quantum Fourier transform

We defined the $n$-qubit quantum Fourier transform as

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_n} = \ket{\varphi_1} \otimes \ket{\varphi_2} \otimes \ldots \otimes \ket{\varphi_n},
$$

for all $b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_n \in \{0,1\}$ where

$$
\ket{\varphi_{n-k}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i \cdot \underbrace{2^k \cdot b}_{0.b_{k+1} \ldots b_n}}\ket{1}\right)
$$

For $n=4$ the QFT circuit looks like this:

![Circuit: 4-qubit QFT with inputs |b_1> to |b_4> reversed by swaps, then Hadamards and controlled R_2, R_3, R_4 rotations; outputs 0.b_4, 0.b_3 b_4, 0.b_2 b_3 b_4, 0.b_1 b_2 b_3 b_4](figures/L10-p06-1.png)

where $R_k$ rotates around the $Z$ axis by angle $2\pi / 2^k$:

$$
R_k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{2\pi i / 2^k} \end{pmatrix}
$$

For example, $R_0 = I$, $R_1 = Z$, $R_2 = S$.

## Discrete Fourier transform

Quantum Fourier transform is a special case of the discrete Fourier transform which exists for any matrix size $M \geq 1$, not just $M = 2^n$:

$$
\mathrm{QFT}(n) = D(2^n)
$$

$D(M)$ is an $M \times M$ complex matrix.

<!-- page 7 -->

The entries of $D(M)$ are as follows:

$$
D_{jk} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} \omega_M^{jk}, \quad j, k \in \{0, \ldots, M-1\}
$$

where $\omega_M = e^{2\pi i / M}$. Note that $\omega_M^M = 1$ and $\bar{\omega}_M = \omega_M^{-1}$. Clearly, $D^\mathsf{T} = D$ since $D_{jk} = D_{kj}$, so $D^\dagger = \bar{D}$ and

$$
(D^\dagger)_{jk} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} \omega_M^{-jk}
$$

We can easily write down the matrix for $D$:

$$
D(M) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\
1 & \omega & \omega^2 & \omega^3 & \cdots & \omega^{M-1} \\
1 & \omega^2 & \omega^4 & \omega^6 & \cdots & \omega^{2(M-1)} \\
1 & \omega^3 & \omega^6 & \omega^9 & \cdots & \omega^{3(M-1)} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
1 & \omega^{M-1} & \omega^{2(M-1)} & \omega^{3(M-1)} & \cdots & \omega^{(M-1)^2}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

It is not obvious that $D$ is unitary (exercise) and that $D(2^n)$ agrees with what is implemented by $\mathrm{QFT}(n)$ (exercise).

The intuition behind the discrete Fourier transform is as follows. The entries in each row of $D$ correspond to points on the unit circle that run around it at increasingly high speeds. If $\ket{y} = D\ket{x}$ then

![Five planes each showing a unit circle with a point at a different position, connected by a grey curve](figures/L10-p07-1.png)

each entry of $\ket{y} \in \C^M$,

$$
y_j = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} \omega^{jk} x_k,
$$

is the inner product of $\ket{x} \in \C^M$ with a spiral of a certain period. This value is large if $\ket{x}$ oscillates with a similar period.
